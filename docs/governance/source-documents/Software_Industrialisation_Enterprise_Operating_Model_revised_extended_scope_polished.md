Executive Position, Intent and Reading Guide

Intent of this paper

This paper defines the enterprise operating model through which COMPANY coordinates, governs and continuously improves Software Industrialisation across its Business Units. Its intent is to make the organisational conditions for shared software-engineering capability explicit: who sets direction, who is accountable, who contributes, which decisions can bind the participating organisations, how shared outcomes are adopted and how progress is measured.

The paper responds to a practical problem. Software responsibility, engineering resources, budgets and delivery commitments are distributed across the Business Units. That distribution is real and must be respected. At the same time, several Software Industrialisation needs cannot be solved efficiently by isolated local initiatives. The purpose of this document is therefore not to create a new central software-development organisation, but to establish the authority and operating mechanisms through which distributed organisations can create coherent enterprise capabilities.

The MDOcore: Problem, Solution Space and Open Challenges paper provides relevant motivation. This document does not reproduce that problem space. It extends the original Software Industrialisation Enterprise Operating Model only where necessary to make it executable and sustainable: funding, competence, benefits, assurance, permanent operation and enterprise interfaces.

Structure and reading logic

The document follows one argument rather than presenting independent governance components. It first establishes the governing context, intent, scope and authority. It then explains the organisational problem, defines the target state and derives the principles that constrain the solution. On that basis it specifies the operating model, governance, accountable roles, leadership and decision rights. The final chapters explain how capabilities progress through their lifecycle, how governance operates and measures results, and how the model is established.

Executive position

Software Industrialisation shall be established as a federated enterprise capability: centrally coordinated, jointly developed and locally executed. Enterprise governance sets direction and decides the capability portfolio. A lightweight Software Industrialisation Office integrates the system. Named Enterprise Capability Leads remain accountable for shared capability outcomes. Business Units, programmes and engineering teams retain accountability for their products, resources, local implementation and delivery.

This allocation is only credible if responsibility is matched by authority. The Steering Board must operate under an explicit executive mandate. Capability Leads require documented decision rights within an approved scope. Business Unit representatives need time, access and authority to secure decisions in their organisations. Business Unit Leads and Engineering Heads must accept the capacity and adoption obligations assigned to them. Without these conditions, enterprise governance becomes voluntary coordination and can be ignored whenever local pressure increases.

Governance alone is insufficient. Every approved capability requires funded capacity, required competence, a named Benefit Owner, proportionate assurance and a defined route to permanent operation or retirement.

**Key message****Enterprise coordination. Federated leadership. Explicit authority. Local execution.**

Why the model is required

Independent local optimisation can create valuable results, but where several Business Units face similar needs it also produces duplicated investment, fragmented practices, uneven maturity, limited reuse and repeated assessment of comparable methods or tools. Full centralisation would create a different failure: it would separate capability development from the product knowledge, customer conditions and delivery accountability held by the Business Units.

The required answer is neither unmanaged local independence nor a central delivery hierarchy. It is a governed federation that provides common direction and enterprise capability while preserving the accountability of the organisations that deliver products and programmes. The model connects the existing distribution of responsibility to explicit enterprise decision rights instead of attempting to conceal or remove it.

The Software Industrialisation Office is accountable for the coherence of the portfolio and governance system. It does not own every technical deliverable and does not allocate Business Unit resources. Enterprise Capability Leads are accountable for their defined outcomes within delegated authority. Business Unit management remains accountable for people, capacity and implementation commitments made by its organisation.

Funding, competence, benefits, risk, assurance and permanent operation are cross-cutting obligations attached to the relevant portfolio, capability and Business Unit decisions.

Distinctive feature: governed federation

The five initial domains are a capability portfolio, not five new organisational silos and not a quota to be distributed one per Business Unit. Leadership shall be assigned according to competence, sustained capacity, enterprise mandate and the dependency structure between the domains. The same Business Unit may host more than one lead where justified and closely coupled domains may share governance or leadership support. In every case, one named person remains accountable for each defined capability outcome.

All materially affected Business Units contribute irrespective of where the lead is hosted. Hosting provides organisational anchoring and capacity; it does not confer exclusive ownership of the capability or a unilateral right to determine enterprise outcomes.

Expected outcomes and immediate decisions

The operating model is intended to achieve the following outcomes:

stronger cross-Business-Unit ownership of shared software-engineering capabilities;

less avoidable duplication of engineering effort and investment;

greater reuse of capabilities, assets and organisational knowledge;

faster and more predictable adoption of common approaches;

transparent enterprise priorities, decision rights and accountabilities;

improved and more even software-engineering maturity;

preserved Business Unit accountability for products and programmes;

continuous organisational learning based on pilots, adoption evidence and feedback;

transparent funding, capacity and benefit ownership; and

sustainable operation, improvement and retirement of permanent capabilities.

Publication alone will not produce these outcomes. Executive governance must approve the model, confirm the Steering Board mandate, establish and resource the Software Industrialisation Office, confirm the five initial capability domains, appoint accountable leads with capacity and deputies, approve the RACI and decision rights, establish the initial funding and benefit model, confirm the required enterprise-function interfaces, and select the first pilots. The first test of the model is whether these commitments remain binding when an enterprise priority conflicts with local budget or schedule pressure.

Strategic Context and Organisational Problem

Chapter 3 defined what this document governs and where its authority stops. This chapter explains why an enterprise response is nevertheless required: which business drivers create shared needs, which organisational conditions obstruct them and why federation is the appropriate response.

Software has become a defining capability of modern products and systems. As software intensity, integration and lifecycle expectations increase, isolated improvements within individual organisational units are no longer sufficient to realise all available enterprise value. COMPANY must be able to improve software engineering across Business Unit boundaries while retaining the local product knowledge and delivery accountability required by different domains and customer environments.

Business drivers

The original Operating Model identifies the following recurring business drivers:

growth in software-intensive and software-defined products;

increasing engineering complexity;

higher cybersecurity, safety, quality and compliance expectations;

longer software lifecycle support;

faster innovation and delivery cycles;

growing dependence on integrated toolchains and platforms;

scarcity of specialised software-engineering expertise; and

greater need for cross-Business-Unit reuse and learning.

These drivers affect Business Units differently, but they create recurring capability needs. Where several units separately assess, develop and maintain similar approaches, COMPANY bears repeated cost and loses opportunities to combine scarce expertise. Where commonality is imposed without regard to local constraints, adoption becomes expensive and may undermine delivery. The model must therefore support deliberate choices about which needs justify an enterprise response.

Current organisational challenges

Business Units own their products, programmes, engineering resources and delivery commitments. That accountability must remain intact. The challenge is that shared software-engineering capability does not emerge automatically from independently governed organisations, even when they face similar needs.

Without coordinated enterprise capability development, the organisation can experience:

independently evolving engineering practices;

duplicated capability and platform investments;

inconsistent governance and assurance;

fragmented organisational knowledge;

uneven maturity;

limited reuse;

repeated local assessment of similar methods and tools;

enterprise priorities without explicit funding, capacity or benefit ownership; and

pilots or shared outcomes without sustainable operating ownership and support.

Local differences are not inherently negative. Some are necessary because products, customers, technologies and constraints differ. The problem is the absence of a transparent enterprise mechanism for distinguishing justified variation from avoidable duplication and for converting shared needs into jointly owned, adoptable capabilities.

Distributed responsibility also creates an authority question. A central coordinating function cannot succeed through facilitation alone if it lacks an executive mandate. Enterprise governance cannot succeed by issuing decisions that Business Units have neither helped shape nor planned to implement. Authority, accountability and participation therefore must be designed as one system.

Why federation

Federation places each type of responsibility at the level best equipped to exercise it. Table 4-1 explains the response to the main organisational needs.

| Effective authority | The Steering Board operates under an executive mandate and delegates defined decisions. |
| Business Unit ownership | Business Units retain products, programmes, resources and local delivery. |
| Enterprise participation | Affected Business Units nominate empowered representatives and provide agreed capacity. |

Enterprise Capability Leads remain accountable within their delegated scope. Business Unit Leads and Engineering Heads remain accountable for the resources and local actions committed by their organisations.

Target State: Vision, Mission and Organisational Outcomes

Chapter 4 established why isolated local optimisation and unsupported central coordination are insufficient. This chapter converts that problem into a target state: the enterprise condition that Software Industrialisation must create and the organisational outcomes against which the model can be judged.

Vision

COMPANY establishes a high-performing federated software-engineering ecosystem in which Business Units jointly develop and benefit from shared enterprise capabilities while retaining ownership of their products, programmes and engineering execution.

The vision does not assume one central software organisation or an identical implementation in every Business Unit. It describes an enterprise in which common needs are visible, shared outcomes have accountable owners, contributions are organised across boundaries and local teams can adopt reusable capabilities with less repeated effort.

Mission

Software Industrialisation establishes, coordinates and continuously improves enterprise software-engineering capabilities by:

aligning capability priorities with enterprise needs;

harmonising practices where commonality creates demonstrable value;

assigning accountable leadership and contribution responsibilities;

enabling cross-Business-Unit collaboration and knowledge exchange;

developing shared capabilities and reusable assets;

supporting pilots, adoption, permanent operation and improvement;

governing funding, competence, benefits, risk and retirement; and

creating transparency over priorities, participation, maturity, investment and results.

The mission is enabling rather than substitutive. Software Industrialisation strengthens the Business Units’ ability to deliver; it does not assume their product responsibility, programme accountability or line-management function.

Purpose of the operating model

The operating model connects enterprise alignment, federated leadership, shared ownership, Business Unit accountability and local execution. These elements cannot be managed independently. Alignment without local accountability produces impractical mandates. Local accountability without enterprise coordination produces repeated investment. Shared ownership without a named lead produces ambiguous decisions. Federated leadership without committed capacity produces governance in name only. Delivery without benefit ownership can produce output without value. Rollout without permanent ownership, funding and support creates an unsupported organisational dependency.

Enterprise Design Principles and Their Operating Consequences

Chapter 5 defined the intended target state. The design principles now translate that target state into mandatory operating choices. They define the organisational logic of Software Industrialisation and guide interpretation where a detailed rule is not provided and shall be applied when governance bodies design forums, allocate leadership, approve roadmaps or resolve disputes. The principles operate as one set: local ownership does not justify ignoring an approved guardrail, and enterprise coordination does not authorise the Office to take over Business Unit delivery.

DP-01 – Coordinate Enterprise Direction while Preserving Local Execution

Strategy, capability portfolio, cross-capability dependencies, governance cadence and executive reporting are coordinated through the Software Industrialisation Office under Steering Board authority. Coordination provides one enterprise view; it does not centralise delivery.

DP-02 – Assign Federated Leadership through Explicit Mandates and Authority

Enterprise capabilities are led by named individuals acting for the enterprise and drawing on Business Unit expertise. Allocation is based on competence, capacity, strategic relevance and dependencies; it does not require one different Business Unit per domain.

DP-03 – Establish Shared Ownership across Participating Business Units

Affected Business Units contribute to development, review, pilots and improvement. Shared ownership means planned contribution and empowered representation, while one named lead remains accountable for the outcome.

DP-04 – Retain Product and Programme Accountability within Local Engineering

Product engineering, programme execution, resource allocation, local implementation and operational delivery remain with the Business Units and programmes. Local governance determines implementation within applicable guardrails.

DP-05 – Provide Enabling Capabilities before Imposing Governance

Governance shall make successful engineering easier through clarity, reusable capabilities, common services and proportionate controls. Adoption effort, training, transition and feedback are part of the outcome.

DP-06 – Standardise Where Commonality Creates Enterprise Value

Harmonisation is introduced where it creates measurable enterprise value. Local variation remains acceptable where justified by product, customer, regulatory or technical constraints. Both commonality and variation require evidence.

DP-07 – Resolve Dependencies through Collaboration before Escalation

Differences are normally resolved through transparent analysis and cross-Business-Unit collaboration. Collaboration does not require unanimity; the accountable owner decides within delegated authority and escalates only where necessary.

DP-08 – Base Decisions on Evidence, Traceability and Measurable Outcomes

Material decisions are supported by a documented need, impact analysis and pilot or engineering evidence where appropriate. Evidence is proportionate to consequence and sufficient to explain rationale, effects and evaluation.

DP-09 – Evolve Capabilities through Feedback and Controlled Improvement

The operating model and capability portfolio evolve through adoption feedback, performance, lessons learned and changing priorities. Change is deliberate and documented; it does not mean continual restructuring.

DP-10 – Secure Funding and Capacity before Accepting Commitments

A committed roadmap item has an approved funding source, named capacity and an accountable budget holder. Unfunded demand may be assessed and prioritised, but it is not represented as committed delivery.

DP-11 – Assign Ownership for Benefits, Risks and the Complete Lifecycle

Every material capability has named ownership for expected benefits and risk, and every permanent capability has an approved operating and retirement model. Accountability continues beyond the first deliverable or rollout.

DP-12 – Self-Service Capabilities – Deliver Enterprise Capabilities through Self-Service and Automation

Enterprise capabilities shall be provided primarily through reusable automation, Infrastructure as Code, Platform as Code, templates, reference implementations and self-service mechanisms.

By enabling projects to independently adopt and operate the capability when required, it forms the foundation for scalable reuse across project boundaries, reducing dependencies on central teams while ensuring consistent standards, processes and outcomes.

Capability teams are accountable for the development, maintenance, governance and evolution of these assets, but shall not become operational intermediaries for routine engineering activities.

Business Units, programmes and engineering teams shall be able to consume approved enterprise capabilities independently within defined guardrails and without creating dependencies on central delivery teams.

Enterprise capabilities shall reduce coordination overhead and avoid the creation of organisational silos or operational bottlenecks.

Software Industrialisation Enterprise Operating Model

Chapter 6 established the principles that constrain the solution. This chapter turns those principles into an executable organisational system: its functional layers, capability portfolio, leadership allocation, cross-boundary exchanges, funding and route into permanent operation. The enterprise operating model describes how Software Industrialisation works as a system. It is not an organisational chart and it does not create a new hierarchy over the Business Units. An organisational structure answers where people report and where disciplinary authority resides. An operating model answers how work crosses those boundaries: who sets direction, who is accountable for a shared outcome, who contributes, which decisions are delegated, how dependencies are resolved and how a capability becomes usable in the Business Units.

This distinction is essential in COMPANY’s distributed structure. The model leaves staff, budgets and delivery accountability in the existing organisation while adding the functional authority and collaboration mechanisms required for coherent enterprise capability.

Operating-model logic

The model follows a recurring operating cycle:

Enterprise governance establishes strategic direction and approves the capability portfolio, funding principles and material investment.

The Software Industrialisation Office integrates the decisions into one roadmap and governance cadence.

Accountable Enterprise Capability Leads organise cross-Business-Unit development within the approved scope.

Business Units and programmes provide needs, expertise, validation environments and adoption capacity.

Capabilities are validated through pilots and introduced through planned adoption.

Mature capabilities transition into funded permanent operation with explicit ownership and support.

Performance, benefit, risk and operational evidence inform improvement and subsequent portfolio decisions.

Capabilities are retired through an approved transition when continued operation is no longer justified.

Each step has a different accountable owner. The Steering Board cannot develop every capability, the Office cannot implement within every programme, and Business Units cannot individually determine what constitutes an enterprise outcome. The operating model creates the interfaces that allow these responsibilities to remain distributed without becoming disconnected.

Enterprise governance

The Chief Digital Officer and Steering Board provide the executive mandate. They approve strategy, the operating model, the capability portfolio, material investment and accountable leadership; review participation, performance and risk; and resolve material cross-Business-Unit escalations. They intervene where enterprise authority is required and delegate routine capability decisions where scope and consequence permit.

Enterprise integration

The Software Industrialisation Lead and Office provide the integration point between enterprise governance, capability development and Business Unit execution. They reconcile portfolio status, dependencies and decision needs so that governance receives a coherent enterprise view and the capability domains receive aligned direction. Section 7.3 defines the Office’s continuing responsibilities and authority limits.

Federated capability development

Each capability domain has one named accountable Enterprise Capability Lead, an appropriate cross-Business-Unit governance arrangement and working groups formed around defined deliverables. The lead organises and remains answerable for the outcome; contributions may come from several Business Units and enterprise functions. Accountability is therefore separated from the location of every contributing resource.

Local engineering execution

Business Units, programmes, projects and engineering teams remain accountable for product delivery and implementation. They make needs and constraints visible, provide agreed expertise and capacity, support validation and pilots, plan adoption, manage local resources and risks, request justified tailoring when required and provide evidence and feedback.

Software Industrialisation Office

The Office is intentionally lightweight. Its effectiveness comes from mandate, access to decision-makers and the quality of integration rather than from accumulating delivery work. Its continuing responsibilities are to:

maintain the integrated enterprise roadmap and capability portfolio view;

coordinate milestones, interdependencies and governance forums;

prepare Steering Board options and decisions;

maintain action, decision, funding, capacity, competence, benefit, risk and performance transparency;

facilitate cross-Business-Unit collaboration and escalation;

coordinate required interfaces with Finance, People / Human Resources and other applicable enterprise functions;

consolidate participation, pilot, adoption and value reporting; and

support periodic review and improvement of the operating model.

Where a capability lacks a lead, a Business Unit has not confirmed capacity or a decision exceeds delegated authority, the Office makes the gap visible and routes it to the accountable body. It does not silently absorb unassigned ownership.

A software factory or factory capability is not, by itself, an organizational structure (“Aufbauorganisation”), a new line department or the central owner of all software development. In this model, the factory-related domain is a governed enterprise capability: a defined set of reusable outcomes, expertise, support and operating responsibilities delivered through the federated organisation. Its staff may remain anchored in existing Business Units and functions; the operating model defines how the capability is owned, developed, adopted and improved across those boundaries.

The domains are interdependent. Controls influence process and evidence; process expectations influence toolchain and platform blueprints; experimental factory outcomes can validate or accelerate those blueprints; and Communication & Rollout supports adoption across all domains. The Office maintains these dependencies in the integrated roadmap.

Leadership and hosting allocation

Leadership is allocated to achieve credible enterprise outcomes, not to distribute one workstream mechanically to each Business Unit. The Steering Board confirms one named accountable Enterprise Capability Lead for every defined domain and the organisational support required for that lead.

The allocation decision considers:

relevant competence and demonstrated experience;

ability to act with an enterprise perspective;

sufficient and sustained capacity;

strategic relevance and enterprise acceptance;

dependencies between capability domains;

access to contributors and validation environments;

authority to coordinate within the delegated scope; and

continuity, deputy and succession arrangements.

The allocation decision records one accountable person, the supporting organisation, committed capacity and applicable decision rights for each roadmap and outcome. Chapter 10 defines the hosting arrangements, leadership review and rotation through which that allocation is sustained.

Interaction between the layers

The model relies on visible exchanges rather than informal hand-offs. Business Units provide needs, evidence, constraints and adoption scope. Enterprise governance approves priorities, investment and delegated authority. Capability Leads organise development, decisions and dependencies. Accepted outcomes are prepared for local adoption. Pilot results, adoption evidence and lessons then flow back into capability improvement and portfolio decisions.

Roadmaps, appointment records, decision logs, pilot records, adoption plans and performance reporting make these exchanges visible. The operating model shall not depend on commitments or decisions known only to individual participants.

Local execution and justified variation

Business Units decide how to organise teams, allocate resources, sequence adoption and integrate a capability into products and programmes where the consequence remains local and the decision stays within applicable guardrails. Local variation is possible, but it shall be explicit where an enterprise capability or guardrail would otherwise apply.

The responsible Business Unit explains the product, customer, regulatory or technical reason, the impact and any treatment required to prevent unnecessary divergence. The applicable decision body decides or reviews the matter within its delegated authority. This avoids treating every difference as non-compliance while preventing local preference from silently becoming repeated enterprise fragmentation.

Transition from workstreams to permanent capabilities

A workstream is an implementation-oriented construct used to establish or materially evolve an enterprise capability. A permanent capability is an enduring responsibility that requires continued ownership, adoption support and improvement. Transition is considered when the scope is stable, accountable ownership and governance exist, principal outcomes are under stewardship, pilots have demonstrated applicability, adoption and support responsibilities are understood, performance can be measured and continuing capacity is agreed.

The Steering Board approves the transition. The cadence or team structure may change, but the principles of enterprise coordination, federated leadership, Business Unit participation and local execution remain. A first deliverable does not make a capability permanent; sustained accountability and capacity do.

Funding, capacity and benefit ownership

An approved priority is not executable until funding and capacity are attached to it. The model therefore distinguishes five funding objects: the Software Industrialisation Office and common governance; development of an enterprise capability; validation and pilots; Business Unit adoption and local implementation; and permanent operation, support, improvement or retirement. The source and accountable budget holder may differ between these objects, but each decision must make the allocation explicit.

Funding may be provided centrally, by participating Business Units or through an approved mixed arrangement. The Steering Board approves enterprise investment and the allocation principle. Business Unit Leads approve local adoption resources. Finance and Controlling validate the cost and benefit assumptions through the applicable BMS process. Unfunded work does not enter the committed roadmap; where an approved priority loses funding or capacity, the Steering Board rebalances, defers or stops it rather than allowing hidden overcommitment.

Every material capability investment has a named Benefit Owner. The Benefit Owner is an existing accountable role appointed for the expected outcome—normally the Enterprise Capability Lead for enterprise capability value and the Business Unit Lead for local adoption value. The investment record defines the problem, beneficiaries, baseline, expected measurable outcome, cost, capacity, time horizon, assumptions and review points. Funding approval and benefit ownership are related but not identical: the budget holder controls expenditure, while the Benefit Owner answers for whether the intended value is evidenced.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Office and common governance | Steering Board through the applicable enterprise budget process | Mandate, staffing, operating cost, planned outputs and effectiveness measures. |
| Capability development | Steering Board for portfolio investment | Approved scope, roadmap, cost, committed capacity, Benefit Owner and expected outcome. |
| Validation and pilot | Capability Lead for selection; budget holders for committed resources | Pilot charter, host, capacity, cost, success criteria and expected decision. |
| Business Unit adoption | Business Unit Lead | Applicability, adoption plan, local resources, dependencies and expected local value. |

Permanent capability operation and retirement

A permanent enterprise capability is operated as a continuing organisational service to defined consumers, even where its people and assets remain distributed across existing organisations. Before transition, the Steering Board approves an operating charter that identifies the accountable Capability Owner, scope and applicability, consumers, provided outcomes or services, support route, operating roles, funding and capacity, dependencies, performance measures, change authority, continuity arrangements and review cadence.

The Capability Owner maintains the capability, coordinates improvement and reports performance. Business Units and programmes remain accountable for local use and implementation. Where an underlying platform or service is operated by another enterprise function, the operating charter records that interface instead of transferring the provider’s existing accountability to the Software Industrialisation organisation.

Retirement is a governed lifecycle decision. It may be proposed when value has disappeared, use has fallen below an agreed threshold, the capability has been superseded, cost or risk is no longer justified, or ownership and support cannot be sustained. Retirement requires impact assessment, consultation with affected Business Units, treatment of dependencies and retained records, a transition or migration plan where required, communication, an effective date and Steering Board approval. Stopping new investment without completing these actions is abandonment, not retirement.

Federated Governance, Mandates and Enterprise Interfaces

Chapter 7 defined how work and accountability move through the operating model. This chapter makes that allocation enforceable by establishing the mandate and authority chain, participation obligations, guardrails, enterprise interfaces, decision evidence and escalation. Governance is effective only when every body knows what it may decide, what it must deliver and what remains outside its authority.

Participation obligations

Federation creates rights and obligations. Business Units can make requirements and constraints visible, contribute to decisions and request justified tailoring. They also provide the participation required to create credible outcomes. Each materially affected Business Unit shall:

nominate representatives with the required expertise and a documented mandate;

confirm competence, time allocation, duration and deputy arrangements;

provide appropriate subject-matter expertise and relevant existing assets;

participate in reviews and decisions within the agreed cadence;

support suitable validation and pilots;

prepare local adoption decisions and plans;

communicate approved outcomes internally;

provide local cost, benefit, risk and performance evidence and lessons learned; and

support permanent capabilities where an approved operating charter assigns a Business Unit contribution.

Attendance alone is insufficient. A representative without capacity, access to local decision-makers or a route to coordinate adoption cannot fulfil the role. Business Unit Leads and Engineering Heads are accountable for creating the necessary conditions. Participation is proportionate: the Capability Lead and Office identify which organisations are materially affected and what contribution is required.

Governance behaviour

Formal authority is necessary but not sufficient. Enterprise leaders act for the enterprise rather than for Business Unit lobbying; issues are supported by evidence and impact analysis; accountable owners decide within their authority instead of seeking unanimity; Business Units raise constraints early; governance distinguishes consultation from approval; material decisions and actions are recorded; and escalation is reserved for matters that exceed delegated authority.

These behaviours allow COMPANY to use distributed expertise without turning distributed accountability into ambiguous accountability.

Guardrails and alignment with enterprise governance

Guardrails establish mandatory boundaries or minimum expectations while leaving implementation freedom inside them. Software Industrialisation Guardrails govern the organisational and capability subjects within this document, such as participation, decision rights, common minimum expectations and justified variation. They exist within the wider enterprise governance system and do not supersede it.

The Architecture Governance Framework remains authoritative for architecture governance and Architecture Guardrails. Software Industrialisation shall therefore follow five alignment rules:

No competing authority: this model creates no second architecture decision body or exception process.

Conformance by design: capability outcomes with architecture implications are developed and accepted consistently with applicable Architecture Guardrails.

Established exception path: deviations from Architecture Guardrails use the Architecture Governance Framework.

Clear scope: Software Industrialisation governance decides organisational and capability matters; architecture and local product decisions remain with their authorised owners.

Consistent terminology and records: subordinate artefacts reference applicable enterprise and architecture decisions where relevant.

The same principle applies to other Enterprise Guardrails: Software Industrialisation may make them usable through capability outcomes, but it shall not duplicate, weaken or contradict their authority.

Within the Software Industrialisation mandate, the following federated guardrails apply:

a hosting organisation cannot unilaterally impose an enterprise outcome outside the approved process;

a participating Business Unit cannot veto progress solely to protect local preference;

the Office coordinates but does not take over technical ownership or Business Unit delivery;

the Steering Board intervenes where enterprise authority is required while routine decisions remain delegated;

local variation remains possible where justified, documented and governed; and

every capability outcome has one named accountable lead even where several organisations contribute.

Organisation, Roles, Accountabilities and RACI

Chapter 7 established the functional layers of the operating model and Chapter 8 established its authority chain. This chapter assigns those accountabilities to defined roles and makes their functional relationships, decision rights, capacity expectations and formal commitments explicit.

This chapter deliberately precedes Chapter 10 on federated leadership, hosting and participation. The organisation must first make accountable roles and authority visible; only then can it describe how distributed leaders should act.

Organisation model

Every participant operates through two concurrent relationships. Disciplinary authority follows the existing organisational structure; functional accountability follows the mandates and decision rights defined in this model. For each material activity, the role being exercised must therefore be explicit. This prevents a functional role from being mistaken for line authority and prevents local line authority from silently displacing an enterprise decision taken within an approved mandate.

Core roles

Chief Digital Officer

Provides executive sponsorship, ensures an enterprise mandate, confirms strategic direction and resolves matters that exceed delegated governance. The role enables federation; it does not manage capability delivery.

Software Industrialisation Steering Board

Acts as the executive decision body for strategy, the operating model, capability portfolio, major investment, leadership allocation, enterprise guardrails within scope and enterprise rollout. Its members must be able to commit their organisations.

Software Industrialisation Lead

Integrates and administers the operating model on behalf of the Steering Board, leads the Office, maintains the enterprise roadmap, aligns capability domains and prepares Steering Board decisions. This operational stewardship does not transfer formal accountability from the Steering Board and gives the role functional coordination authority, but not Business Unit line authority.

Software Industrialisation Office

Provides portfolio management, governance integration, enterprise-interface coordination, dependency management, facilitation, decision preparation and reporting. It maintains the common view of commitments, funding, capacity, competence, roadmaps, risks, benefits, pilots, adoption, permanent capabilities and performance.

Business Unit Lead

Retains organisational accountability and confirms the Business Unit’s participation, authorised representatives, local funding and capacity, pilots, adoption, local benefit ownership and applicable permanent-capability obligations. The role ensures that commitments made for the Business Unit are resourced and enforceable.

Business Unit Engineering Head

Connects enterprise capability development to local engineering execution. The role confirms engineering competence and capacity, supports pilot selection, assesses adoption feasibility, governs justified local tailoring and ensures that local engineering risks and evidence are handled within applicable guardrails.

Enterprise Capability Lead

Leads a defined workstream or capability for the enterprise, maintains its roadmap and benefit plan, coordinates cross-Business-Unit development, manages risks and dependencies, prepares decisions and supports transition from pilot through adoption to permanent operation or retirement.

Business Unit Representative

Acts as the authorised interface between a capability and a Business Unit. The representative contributes needs and expertise, coordinates input, supports pilots, prepares adoption and reports local cost, benefit, risk, performance and lessons.

Working Group Lead

Is accountable for a defined work package or specialist outcome, organises contributors and reports results, risks and decisions to the Enterprise Capability Lead. The role has no independent enterprise authority unless explicitly delegated.

Programme and Engineering Management

Implements applicable capabilities in product and programme contexts and remains accountable for local planning, team organisation, engineering execution and delivery. Programmes also provide the environments in which capabilities are piloted.

Benefit Owner and permanent Capability Owner

These are explicit accountability designations assigned to an existing authorised role, not additional line positions. The Benefit Owner answers for evidencing an agreed outcome. The permanent Capability Owner answers for the operating charter, continued support, performance, improvement and retirement proposal after transition. The Steering Board records both assignments and may assign them to the same person where appropriate.

Competence, capacity and continuity

An appointment is not complete when only a name is provided. Every appointment shall record the role holder and organisational home, mandate and decision rights, required competence and relevant experience, expected time allocation and duration, objectives and deliverables, identified competence gaps and development actions, deputy or continuity arrangement, sponsoring Business Unit Lead and Engineering Head where applicable, and the route for resolving capacity or authority conflicts.

Capacity commitments are management commitments. If local delivery pressure makes an agreed contribution impossible, the conflict is resolved by the responsible Business Unit leadership and, where required, the Steering Board. It is not left to an under-authorised representative.

Capability Leads define the competence needed for their roadmaps and identify critical skill or continuity risks. Business Unit Engineering Heads confirm the availability and suitability of nominated specialists. People / Human Resources supports workforce planning, learning and formal development mechanisms. The Office maintains an enterprise view of critical capability gaps, but does not become the disciplinary owner of the people involved.

Where required competence is unavailable, the accountable owner must choose an explicit treatment: develop internal competence, obtain temporary specialist support, source an external contribution, change scope or sequence, or escalate the unmet dependency. Appointing an unqualified or unavailable person does not close the gap.

Federated Leadership, Hosting and Participation

Chapter 9 defined the accountable roles and their decision rights. This chapter specifies how Enterprise Capability Leads and Business Unit Representatives are appointed, expected to act, organisationally supported and periodically reviewed. Federated leadership succeeds when people remain anchored in their existing organisations while exercising an explicit enterprise mandate.

Enterprise Capability Lead

The Enterprise Capability Lead is nominated through the agreed hosting arrangement and formally confirmed by the Steering Board. The individual remains organisationally anchored in an existing structure but performs the role for all participating Business Units. The Lead is accountable for the enterprise outcome, not for promoting the preferences of the host organisation.

Accountabilities

The Enterprise Capability Lead shall:

maintain the capability roadmap and its link to the integrated roadmap;

coordinate cross-Business-Unit development and assigned working groups;

ensure that materially affected Business Units are represented and their constraints understood;

prepare recommendations and decisions in accordance with the decision framework;

manage risks, dependencies, actions and commitments within delegated authority;

maintain the approved funding, capacity and benefit assumptions with the assigned owners;

report progress, outcomes and unresolved issues through the defined forums;

maintain the evidence required to accept deliverables and evaluate pilots;

support transition from pilot to rollout, Business Unit adoption and permanent operation;

prepare the operating charter, improvement or retirement recommendation where applicable; and

preserve continuity, decisions and stewardship as the capability evolves.

These accountabilities do not create unrestricted authority. Enterprise rollout, major investment, material enterprise-risk acceptance, transition to permanent status, retirement and changes to enterprise guardrails remain Steering Board decisions. Local implementation and product delivery remain with Business Units and programmes.

Required behaviours

The Lead demonstrates an enterprise mindset, neutrality when facilitating competing needs, transparency about evidence and dissent, the ability to resolve conflict without allowing consensus-seeking to stop progress, disciplined use of delegation and escalation, respect for local engineering knowledge and ownership of follow-through after a pilot or deliverable.

A technically strong nominee who lacks time, authority or the ability to work across organisational boundaries is not an effective appointment.

Hosting organisation

Each capability requires an explicit organisational home for the Lead, capacity and continuity. A hosting Business Unit or enterprise function provides agreed time, subject-matter support and succession. Hosting does not confer exclusive ownership of the capability, its artefacts or its decisions.

The Steering Board may place related domains under a coherent leadership arrangement, assign more than one domain to the same host, use a jointly staffed arrangement or separate an over-broad workstream. In every case, the accountable person, roadmap and decision rights remain explicit.

Business Unit Representatives

Representatives are co-creators, not observers. They articulate Business Unit needs, contribute expertise and reusable assets, review proposals, identify constraints, coordinate pilot access, prepare adoption with local leadership, report progress and value, and bring unresolved local issues into capability governance.

Representatives do not have an unlimited veto to preserve local preference. Equally, consultation cannot be reduced to a formality followed by an outcome imposed outside delegated authority.

Leadership allocation, review and rotation

Leadership assignments are reviewed periodically and when scope, maturity, capacity or context changes materially. The review considers roadmap performance, participation, capacity, unresolved dependencies, enterprise acceptance, continuity and whether the current workstream structure still reflects the capability being developed.

Rotation is an option, not a goal. It may broaden ownership or address a change in capability focus, but every change requires a prepared handover of roadmap, decisions, actions, risks, relationships and evidence.

Failure modes to avoid

using an enterprise role primarily for Business Unit lobbying;

appointing a lead without sufficient time, authority or a deputy;

assuming that each domain must be led by a different Business Unit;

splitting strongly dependent domains into artificial organisational silos;

allowing the Office to take over delivery because federated roles are under-resourced;

appointing representatives who cannot secure Business Unit commitments;

treating consultation as a requirement for unanimity;

operating through informal agreements without decision records;

committing roadmap work without approved funding, capacity or competence;

measuring delivery activity without assigning benefit ownership;

transitioning a workstream without an operating charter and recurring support;

accepting material risk outside delegated authority; and

leaving accountability unclear when a workstream changes, becomes permanent or is retired.

Decision Rights, Escalation and Decision Records

Chapters 9 and 10 established who is accountable and how federated leaders exercise their mandate. This chapter converts that allocation into a usable decision system: which level decides, who participates, when escalation is required and how the decision remains traceable. The decision framework prevents enterprise coordination from becoming centralised micro-management and prevents local autonomy from blocking coherent enterprise outcomes. Decisions are taken at the lowest level with sufficient authority to manage their impact. The approved RACI and documented delegations determine accountability.

Decision principles

decide at the lowest appropriate level;

preserve Business Unit and programme accountability for local delivery;

protect approved enterprise and architecture guardrails;

use evidence and impact analysis proportionate to the decision;

consult the parties that must implement or are materially affected;

document material decisions and their owners;

escalate only where delegated authority is insufficient; and

avoid consensus paralysis by making the accountable decision owner explicit.

Decision categories

Enterprise decisions

Enterprise decisions establish or change the common mandate: strategy, the Operating Model, capability portfolio, funding and major investment, leadership allocation, material enterprise risk acceptance, enterprise guardrails within scope, enterprise rollout, transition to permanent operation and retirement. The Steering Board owns these decisions because their effects extend across Business Units.

Capability decisions

Capability decisions shape work within an approved mandate: roadmap, priorities, deliverable acceptance, pilot selection, benefit-realisation activity, capability risk treatment and improvement. The Enterprise Capability Lead is accountable through the Capability Board and within the boundaries delegated by the Steering Board.

Business Unit and local decisions

Local decisions concern participation, resources, adoption, implementation, team organisation and justified tailoring within applicable guardrails, local risk and local benefit evidence. They remain with Business Unit and programme roles because those roles carry the associated delivery accountability.

Consensus and escalation

Capability decisions should normally be reached through informed consensus because affected Business Units hold essential knowledge. Consensus means that relevant perspectives and evidence have been considered; it does not give every participant an unlimited veto.

- The issue owner documents the decision required, options, evidence and expected impacts.
- The Enterprise Capability Lead attempts resolution within the Capability Board and delegated mandate.
- The Software Industrialisation Office facilitates where another capability, the integrated roadmap or several Business Units are affected.
- The Steering Board decides only where the matter is strategic, materially cross-Business-Unit or beyond delegated authority.

The normal path is Working Group → Capability Board → Software Industrialisation Office / Portfolio Review → Steering Board. An escalation identifies the decision requested and the consequence of delay; it is not a substitute for incomplete analysis.

Decision records

Material decisions are retained in a common decision log. Each record includes the decision and category, accountable owner, date and scope, options, evidence and rationale, consulted Business Units or programmes, financial and capacity implications, expected benefits, risks and dependencies, actions and due dates, applicable conditions or review date, and any later escalation or superseding decision.

The record should be concise enough to maintain consistently and complete enough to avoid recurring debate about what was decided, by whom and with what effect.

Capability Lifecycle, Adoption and Engagement

Chapter 11 defined how decisions are taken. This chapter applies those rights to the complete capability lifecycle, from an identified need through development, adoption, permanent operation, improvement and retirement. The engagement model describes how an identified need becomes a usable and adopted enterprise capability. It prevents Software Industrialisation from becoming either a collection of centrally produced artefacts with no users or a sequence of unrelated local initiatives with no enterprise reuse.

Engagement is continuous. Business Units and programmes participate in identifying needs, assessing relevance, developing outcomes, providing pilot environments, planning adoption and improving the capability. The level of effort is proportionate to scope and risk, but each stage produces a clear outcome and accountable next step.

Intake and assessment

Needs may originate from Business Units, programmes, enterprise strategy, customer or regulatory requirements, lessons learned, architecture, security, quality, safety or technology developments. The route shall be visible and accessible; a need should not depend on personal access to the Office or an informal agreement with a Capability Lead.

Assessment is comparative. It considers enterprise relevance, the number and significance of affected Business Units, expected value, urgency and risk, reuse potential, implementation effort, dependencies and pilot readiness. A high-value need may still be deferred when critical capacity or a suitable pilot is unavailable; the condition and route to readiness are recorded.

Prioritisation determines whether the need enters the approved portfolio, is handled within an existing capability, is combined with a similar need, is deferred or is rejected. Every accepted item receives an accountable lead and visible place in the integrated roadmap.

Cross-Business-Unit development

For a prioritised need, the Capability Lead establishes a team or time-bound working group with a clear problem, named contributors and capacity, expected outputs and acceptance criteria, relevant Business Unit constraints, dependencies, target dates and a reporting and decision route.

The lead coordinates delivery while requirements, assumptions and proposed outcomes remain transparent. Common outcomes preserve necessary local implementation freedom. Where an element should remain local, the reason is made explicit instead of being discovered only during rollout.

Validation is part of development. Review by representatives, programmes and relevant specialists tests whether the outcome is understandable, usable and ready for a real pilot. Validation does not replace the pilot; it prevents unresolved scope or quality questions from being transferred into the pilot environment.

Pilot model

A pilot is a controlled implementation in a real programme or engineering environment. Its purpose is to obtain evidence about applicability, value, risk and adoption effort before an enterprise rollout decision. Each pilot begins with a short charter that identifies the capability version, host Business Unit and programme, accountable roles, context and duration, success criteria, capacity, constraints, evidence to collect and the decision expected at completion.

Success criteria are agreed before execution. The pilot report records what was implemented, the result against each criterion, effort, risks, lessons and the recommendation to scale, revise or stop. A pilot does not automatically justify rollout; the Capability Lead and Office also consider whether the environment is representative and whether other Business Units can adopt the outcome.

Adoption model

An approved rollout makes a capability available or applicable across a defined scope. It does not remove the need for Business Unit adoption planning. The standard path is:

- Early-adopter pilot in a real environment.
- Validation and refinement using the pilot evidence.
- A rollout package containing the accepted outcome, applicability, implementation guidance, support material, known constraints and feedback route.
- Business Unit adoption planning for affected programmes, sequence, resources, dependencies and justified tailoring.
- Staged implementation in manageable increments.
- Transition to normal Business Unit and programme ownership.
- Performance and feedback review to confirm use, value and improvement needs.

The Business Unit Lead approves the local adoption plan; Programme and Engineering Management implements it; the representative coordinates with the capability and reports status. Where a capability is not applicable, that conclusion and its rationale are recorded so that justified scope is distinguishable from unreported non-adoption.

Communication and change

Communication & Rollout is a dedicated capability domain because adoption requires more than publication of a deliverable. It coordinates stakeholder mapping, purpose and timing, training and supporting material, communities and local champions, adoption support, feedback, decisions, progress and evidence-based lessons.

Communication begins during need identification and development. Early involvement helps affected teams understand the problem, influence the outcome and prepare capacity. Communications shall distinguish clearly between proposals, pilots, approved enterprise capabilities and locally adopted outcomes.

Suppliers and partners

Where a capability affects suppliers or partners, the responsible workstream identifies the effect during assessment and development. Engagement defines applicable expectations and interfaces, the point at which the external party must contribute or adopt, transition periods, required evidence and the support available. COMPANY accountability remains with the roles defined in this model.

The lifecycle is supported by a small connected record set: need and intake record; assessment and prioritisation decision; working-group mandate; deliverable acceptance; pilot charter and report; rollout decision and package; Business Unit adoption plan and status; funding and benefit record; capability risk record; permanent operating charter; performance and improvement actions; and, where applicable, retirement decision and closure evidence. The records reuse information and preserve accountability; documentation volume is not an outcome.

Governance Forums and Operating Cadence

Chapter 12 established the lifecycle and the evidence produced at each stage. This chapter defines the recurring forums through which that evidence is reviewed and the required decisions are made. Governance forums create the operating rhythm that connects strategy, portfolio coordination, federated leadership and operational feedback. A forum does not acquire authority merely because it meets regularly. Its authority follows the roles and decision rights defined in Chapters 9 and 11.

Steering Board

The Steering Board approves strategy, portfolio, major investment and funding principles, leadership allocation, material risk treatment or acceptance, enterprise rollout, transition to permanent operation, retirement and material Operating Model changes; reviews integrated performance, benefits and maturity; and resolves strategic or material cross-Business-Unit escalation. Its agenda focuses on matters requiring executive authority rather than detailed capability coordination.

SI Portfolio Review

The Portfolio Review is the main integration forum. Chaired by the Software Industrialisation Lead, it aligns capability roadmaps, identifies dependencies, reviews capacity, funding, benefits and risk, agrees coordination actions within the approved portfolio and prepares matters that require Steering Board decisions.

Capability Boards

Each capability has a federated Board chaired by its accountable Lead. Materially affected Business Units are represented. The Board reviews progress, benefits and risks; prioritises work within delegation; accepts deliverables; selects and monitors pilots; coordinates adoption and permanent operation; and prepares decisions that exceed its authority. Closely related domains may share a Board if accountability remains clear for every outcome.

Working groups

A working group develops a defined work package. Its mandate states the lead, participants, capacity, outputs, acceptance criteria, dates and reporting route. The group is dissolved or re-chartered when complete; it does not become an informal permanent governance body.

Communities of Practice

Communities support informal learning, mentoring, exchange of patterns and early feedback. They help identify needs and improve adoption, but they do not replace formal representation or make governed decisions. Matters requiring a decision enter through intake or a Business Unit representative.

Meeting discipline

Formal forums use a published purpose and agenda, timely decision material, a clear distinction between information, discussion and decision items, named owners, decision and action logs, evidence-based reporting, explicit escalation and periodic effectiveness review. An item should be handled once at the lowest forum with adequate authority; higher-level reporting summarises the decision, impact and exception rather than repeating the working discussion.

Performance, Benefits, KPIs and Maturity

Chapter 13 established the governance cadence. This chapter defines the evidence those forums use to determine whether the operating model, its capabilities and their adoption are producing value. Performance management determines whether the operating model creates enterprise value and becomes sustainable. It is not intended to reward the production of meetings, documents or controls. The measures focus on strategic alignment, active federation, usable capability delivery, adoption, engineering value and organisational improvement.

Measurement approach

A KPI is useful only when it supports a decision. Before first use, its owner shall record the management question, precise definition and scope, calculation or assessment method, controlled source, data and reporting owners, baseline and target, cadence, known limitations and the action expected when the result falls outside the agreed range.

Targets are approved with the relevant roadmap or adoption plan. Ratios use only items that are due or applicable in the reporting period; exclusions are transparent. Where value cannot be expressed credibly as a number, a defined evidence-based assessment is preferable to false precision. The catalogue below is the proposed enterprise baseline and shall be validated jointly with the Business Units before formal target values are set.

Benefit and value management

Performance of the operating model, delivery of a capability and realisation of business or engineering value are different questions. A workstream may deliver on time but create no adopted outcome; a capability may be adopted but fail to produce the expected benefit. Reporting shall therefore distinguish activity and output measures, adoption and use measures, service or operational measures, and realised benefit measures.

For every material investment, the Benefit Owner maintains a proportionate benefit-realisation plan containing the approved problem and beneficiaries, baseline, expected outcome and target, attribution assumptions, required adoption, cost and capacity, measurement source, review points and the decision that follows the review. Benefits claimed by more than one capability or Business Unit are reconciled to prevent double counting.

Benefit reviews support explicit decisions: continue as approved, scale, revise scope or support, change the target or measurement where the original assumption was invalid, defer, or stop and retire. A missed target is not automatically failure, but it requires an evidence-based decision rather than silent continuation.

Performance review and refinement

The Office maintains the enterprise scorecard and reviews data quality with contributing owners. Implementation Roadmap and Exit Criteria

Chapter 14 defined how progress, benefits and maturity are evaluated. This chapter converts the target operating model into a phased establishment path and uses evidence-based exit criteria to prevent calendar progress from being mistaken for organisational capability. Software Industrialisation is established incrementally. COMPANY first creates the authority, roles and limited portfolio required to prove federation; then develops and pilots shared outcomes; integrates proven capabilities into Business Unit operation; and improves the system through evidence. The phases are maturity steps rather than calendar claims.

Phase 1 – Establish

The purpose of Phase 1 is to make the federated model operational. Principal actions are:

approve the Operating Model and the Steering Board mandate;

establish and resource the Software Industrialisation Office;

confirm the five initial capability domains and coherent governance arrangements;

appoint Enterprise Capability Leads and Business Unit representatives;

confirm authority, capacity, deputies and hosting arrangements;

approve initial capability and integrated roadmaps;

establish the funding categories, initial budget and capacity commitments;

assign Benefit Owners and establish initial benefit baselines;

confirm enterprise-function interfaces and risk and assurance responsibilities;

establish forums, decision, risk and competence records and the performance baseline;

obtain Business Unit Lead and Engineering Head acknowledgement of the RACI; and

select the first pilots.

Exit condition: required roles are filled and acknowledged, forums operate, roadmaps and decision rights, funding and capacity are approved, enterprise interfaces are confirmed, and the first pilots have named hosts, benefit expectations and success criteria.

Phase 2 – Harmonise

The purpose of Phase 2 is to develop the first coherent outcomes and validate them across Business Unit contexts. Principal actions are:

develop the first capability deliverables;

execute cross-Business-Unit reviews and pilots;

distinguish common elements from justified local implementation;

capture pilot evidence, adoption effort and lessons;

validate cost, benefit, risk and competence assumptions;

refine governance, engagement and decision practices;

build Communities of Practice and adoption support; and

prepare reusable rollout packages for successful capabilities.

Exit condition: planned pilots are reported, reusable capability packages exist, common and local elements are understood and initial cost, benefit and risk assumptions are tested with evidence.

Phase 3 – Industrialise

The purpose of Phase 3 is to move successful capabilities from isolated pilots into stable and repeatable use. Principal actions are:

approve enterprise rollout where pilot evidence supports it;

integrate applicable capabilities into Business Unit and programme planning;

execute staged Business Unit adoption;

establish stable ownership, support, funding and competence continuity;

approve operating charters for permanent capabilities;

monitor adoption, benefits, operational performance and risk; and

transition mature workstreams into permanent enterprise capabilities.

Exit condition: applicable Business Units have approved and executed adoption plans, permanent capabilities have approved operating charters, ownership and capacity are stable, and measurable value is visible beyond the initial pilots.

Phase 4 – Optimise

The purpose of Phase 4 is to improve the value and efficiency of the established model. Principal actions are:

use performance, feedback and maturity evidence to prioritise improvement;

review benefit cases, operating costs and material risks;

retire low-value activities and capabilities through the governed retirement process;

expand scope only where demonstrated value justifies it;

renew or rotate leadership where competence, capacity or continuity requires it;

strengthen integration and automation within the existing capability portfolio; and

review the balance between enterprise coordination and local execution.

Phase 4 is continuous. Its evidence is the repeated conversion of lessons, benefit, cost, risk and performance findings into decisions, improved outcomes and controlled retirement of activity that no longer provides sufficient value.

The Office maintains the integrated roadmap and phase-exit evidence. The Portfolio Review examines progress and dependencies monthly. The Steering Board reviews progress quarterly and approves material changes, enterprise rollout, transition into permanent capabilities and retirement. A phase exit is a governance decision supported by evidence, not a date on a plan.

Glossary and Defined Terms

The preceding chapters use a deliberately controlled vocabulary to distinguish line organisation, functional accountability, capability ownership and governance authority. This final chapter defines those terms so that the model can be applied consistently in subordinate artefacts, appointments, decisions and operating charters.

**Accountability** The obligation to answer for an outcome and ensure that the required decision or work is completed. Each RACI activity in this model has exactly one accountable role.

**Architecture Guardrail** A mandatory architecture boundary governed through the Architecture Governance Framework. Software Industrialisation does not create a competing authority for it.

**Assurance** The proportionate and, where required, independent evaluation of whether applicable obligations, evidence and conditions have been satisfied.

**Benefit Owner** An existing authorised role explicitly assigned accountability for defining, measuring and reporting whether an approved investment produces its intended outcome.

**Business Unit** An organisational unit that retains accountability for products, programmes, people, resources and engineering execution.

**Business Unit Engineering Head** The leader accountable for connecting enterprise capability development with the Business Unit’s engineering organisation, capacity, adoption feasibility and justified local tailoring.

**Business Unit Lead** The leader accountable for confirming the Business Unit’s participation, capacity and organisational commitments under this model.

**Business Unit Representative** A person authorised to co-create, review, pilot and support adoption of enterprise capabilities for a Business Unit.

**Capability Board** The federated governance forum for the roadmap, priorities, deliverables, pilots, adoption, operational performance, risks, benefits and improvement of a defined capability.

**Capability Portfolio** The approved set of Software Industrialisation workstreams and permanent enterprise capabilities governed through the integrated roadmap.

**Community of Practice** A voluntary network for knowledge exchange, mentoring and learning without formal decision authority.

**Decision Right** The formally assigned authority to make a specified class of decision within a defined boundary.

**Enterprise Capability** A persistent organisational capability serving multiple or all Business Units.

**Enterprise Capability Lead** The person formally appointed to lead a defined workstream or capability on behalf of the enterprise.

**Enterprise Guardrail** A mandatory enterprise boundary or minimum expectation that leaves implementation freedom inside it.

**Enterprise Interface** A governed relationship with an existing enterprise function whose contribution or authority is required by a Software Industrialisation capability.

**Experimental Factory Standards** The original capability domain for shared accelerators, reference implementations, reusable assets and demonstrators. It is not, by itself, a new line organisation.

**Federated Leadership** A model in which leadership is distributed across existing organisations while enterprise coordination, accountability and decision rights remain explicit.

**Funding Object** A defined category of expenditure and capacity for which an accountable budget decision and evidence are required.

**Hosting Arrangement** The organisational arrangement that provides a Capability Lead with disciplinary anchoring, capacity, support and continuity without conferring exclusive ownership.

**Local Execution** The principle that Business Units, programmes, projects and engineering teams remain accountable for delivery and implementation.

**Operating Charter** The approved definition of scope, ownership, consumers, provided outcomes or services, funding, support, measures, change rights and continuity for a permanent capability.

**Operating Model** The integrated description of organisation, governance, roles, decision rights, funding, competence, engagement, operation, assurance, performance and improvement.

**Permanent Capability Owner** The existing authorised role assigned accountability for the operating charter, support, performance, improvement and retirement proposal of a permanent capability.

**Pilot** A controlled implementation used to validate applicability, value, risk and adoption effort in a real environment.

**RACI** A responsibility-assignment method identifying who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed.

**Residual Risk** Risk remaining after planned treatment and requiring acceptance by the role authorised for its consequence.

**Self-Service Capability**An enterprise capability delivered through reusable automation, Infrastructure as Code, Platform as Code, templates or standardised services that can be consumed directly by engineering teams within approved guardrails without requiring routine intervention by the responsible capability team.

**Shared Ownership** The principle that participating Business Units contribute to and benefit from enterprise capabilities while retaining local delivery accountability.

**Software Factory** A governed enterprise capability that provides shared outcomes, expertise and support for repeatable software engineering. It is not, by definition, a department or disciplinary organisational structure.

**Software Industrialisation Office** The lightweight enterprise function responsible for portfolio coordination, governance integration, dependency management, facilitation and reporting.

**Software Industrialisation Steering Board** The executive body responsible for strategy, capability portfolio, major investment, material enterprise risk, leadership allocation, permanent capability status, enterprise decisions and performance.

**Workstream** An implementation-oriented construct used to establish or materially evolve an Enterprise Capability; it is not necessarily a permanent organisational unit.

**Working Group** A time-bound group established to produce a defined work package under a named Working Group Lead.
